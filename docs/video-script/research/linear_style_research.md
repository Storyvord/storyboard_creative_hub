# Linear-Style Demo Video Playbook for Storyvord Creative Hub

A scriptwriter's reference, distilled from studying Linear, Notion, Figma, Arc, Vercel, Loom, Superhuman, Frame.io, StudioBinder, and Milanote. Lift these principles directly into the Creative Hub script.

---

## 1. Opening Hooks That Work

You have roughly **8 seconds** before a viewer decides whether to stay. Never open with the product name, logo animation, or "Welcome to Storyvord." Those are the most expensive seconds of the video — spend them on the audience, not yourself.

The five hook archetypes that consistently work in top SaaS demos:

1. **The named pain.** Start by describing a moment the audience has lived a hundred times. Loom's classic opener is a character staring at a calendar full of meetings while a colleague asks, "Why don't you replace some of those meetings with a Loom?" The audience nods before the product is even named.
2. **The cold-open product moment.** Superhuman uses zero voiceover for its first beats — just the UI doing something impossibly fast. The viewer thinks, "Wait, what just happened?" and now they're watching.
3. **The provocation.** Linear's site lead — *"The system for modern product development"* — works because it implies the alternative is broken. A demo version: "Most filmmakers still run pre-production out of a spreadsheet, a Slack channel, and twelve unread emails. That's about to look ridiculous."
4. **The reclaimed promise.** Linear's *Method* essay opens with *"There is a lost art of building true quality software."* Nostalgic + aspirational + a quiet challenge. For filmmakers: "There's a lost art of pre-production. The moment a film actually starts."
5. **The before/after montage.** Three quick cuts: chaos → product → clarity. No voice for the first 4 seconds. Music does the work.

**Rule of thumb:** the first sentence of voice should never contain the word "Storyvord." Earn the name by second 20.

## 2. Beat Sheet for a 60-120 Second Chapter

Treat every feature chapter as a tiny film. The proven structure:

1. **Tension (5-10s)** — One sentence naming the specific friction this chapter solves. "Casting used to mean three browser tabs, a PDF, and someone's WhatsApp."
2. **Reveal (3-5s)** — Cut to the product solving it. No narration over the first second of the reveal. Let the cut land.
3. **Demo flow (25-40s)** — Walk a real workflow, not a feature tour. One continuous, plausible task. Voice describes *intent*, not clicks.
4. **The killer detail (10-15s)** — The one thing that earns a "wait, really?" from the viewer. A keyboard shortcut, an automation, an AI assist. Slow down here. Hold on the screen 2 beats longer than feels natural.
5. **Workflow placement (5-10s)** — One sentence locating this feature in the bigger picture. "And every change here flows straight into the call sheet you'll send tomorrow."

Linear's demo follows this almost exactly: name a problem ("project management tools are slow"), reveal the speed, walk through issue creation, hit the keyboard-shortcut beat, then locate the feature inside the roadmap-to-ship arc.

## 3. Voice and Tone for a Filmmaker Audience

Filmmakers are creative professionals who can smell SaaS marketing copy from a mile away. They use words like *call sheet*, *recce*, *strip*, *one-liner*, *coverage*, *block*, *DOP*, *first AD*. Use those words. Drop "optimize," "leverage," "streamline," "solution," "platform," "stakeholder," "enterprise-grade."

**Tonal target:** the voice of a senior producer giving a quiet walkthrough to a first-time director. Confident, calm, slightly dry. Never hyped. Never apologetic.

**Words to use:** *prep, locking, the day, the shoot, on set, talent, locations, schedule, the cut, the brief, the script, run a shoot, ship the film.*

**Words to avoid:** *users, stakeholders, workflows (use "the way you work"), solutions, dashboards (use "your board" or "your day"), seamless, robust, end-to-end, holistic, unlock, empower.*

**Sentence shape:** short. Concrete nouns. Active verbs. Avoid adjective stacking. Compare:

- Bad: "Our intuitive, AI-powered platform empowers production teams to seamlessly collaborate."
- Good: "You write the scene list. Creative Hub turns it into a shoot schedule."

Linear's *Method* page is a masterclass — every bullet is an imperative verb ("Set," "Prioritize," "Generate," "Build"). Copy that grammar.

## 4. Pacing and Length

- **Narration speed: 135-150 WPM.** Anything faster sounds like a podcast ad; anything slower sounds like a corporate training video. For a filmmaker audience aim for the low end of that range — these are people who appreciate breathing room.
- **Per-chapter length: 60-90s for a feature chapter, 30-45s for a transition or wrap.** The whole demo should land between **3:30 and 6:00**. Anything longer is a webinar.
- **Words per chapter at 140 WPM:** ~140 words for a 60s chapter, ~210 for 90s. Write to that budget. Cut anything that isn't earning its second.
- **Silence is a feature.** Plan 2-3 deliberate pauses per chapter — at the reveal, at the killer detail, and at the workflow-placement line. The image carries the meaning during the pause.
- **Cut fast on lists and setup. Cut slow on payoff.** The "wait, really?" moment needs an extra beat of hold time.

---

## 5. Show, Don't Tell

The single biggest tell of an amateur demo is narration that reads the screen back to the viewer. If the button on screen says "Generate Call Sheet," the voiceover does not say "click Generate Call Sheet." It says "and the call sheet is ready" — or it says nothing at all.

Operating rules:

- **Narrate intent, not action.** "We're locking the schedule" beats "I'm clicking the Lock button."
- **Let the cursor be silent.** Cursor movements, hovers, and clicks have their own micro-rhythm. Don't talk over them.
- **One idea per shot.** If the screen is doing two things, the voice does zero things.
- **Numbers and outcomes belong on screen, not in narration.** If the AI generated 47 shots in 8 seconds, put "47 shots · 8 seconds" as a chyron. Don't say it.
- **The "earned silence" rule:** after a killer detail, give the viewer 1-2 seconds with no voice. That's where they decide to share the video.

## 6. Transitions Between Chapters

The reason Linear's demo feels like one film and not eight ads is that every transition answers the question *"and then what happened?"* — not *"and here's another feature."*

Three transition patterns to steal:

1. **The artifact handoff.** End chapter A with the output of that feature visible on screen. Start chapter B by picking that artifact up. "Here's the locked schedule. And the moment it locks, the call sheet writes itself." The schedule literally morphs into the call sheet.
2. **The time jump.** "Three days later, you're on set." A single line that moves the story forward in production time. Filmmakers think in days-to-shoot, so use that calendar.
3. **The character handoff.** "The director sees the same board. So does the first AD. So does the line producer." One feature, three perspectives, no new screen needed. Notion uses this constantly.

Avoid: "Next, let's look at..." "Another great feature is..." "You can also..." These signal a feature list and break the spell.

## 7. CTA and Outro Without Feeling Salesy

Top demos end the way good films end — with a small, quiet line and a card. Not a pitch.

Patterns that work:

- **The earned tagline.** Hold on the product hero shot. One line of voice: "Creative Hub. The way pre-production should feel." Fade.
- **The invitation, not the sell.** "Open Creative Hub. Start with one scene. See how far it takes you." This respects the viewer's intelligence.
- **The credits card.** Logo, URL, one line. No "Sign up for a free 14-day trial now!" — that signals a webinar, not a film.
- **The community nod.** Arc's outro implies you're joining people who already get it. For filmmakers: "Built with directors, producers, and ADs who were tired of the old way."

Never end with: a pricing slide, a feature recap list, a discount code, a talking-head founder.

## 8. Specific Linear Techniques Worth Stealing

- **Keyboard shortcut as flex.** When Linear demos a shortcut, the keys appear on-screen as a small overlay (e.g. `⌘ K`). It's a visual brag — "this product is fast enough to need shortcuts." Creative Hub equivalent: show the AI prompt or natural-language command in an overlay as it's typed.
- **The opinionated voice.** Linear says things like *"Write issues, not user stories"* — small declarative manifestos. We can do the same: "Don't manage your shoot. Run it." or "Stop writing call sheets. Lock the schedule."
- **Speed as a character.** Linear's demos are physically fast. Cuts happen on the beat of the action. Latency is the villain. For Creative Hub: every AI generation should feel instant, even if you have to cut around the wait.
- **The product is the protagonist.** No talking heads. No founder cameo until maybe the end. The UI is on screen 95% of the time.
- **Quiet color, loud type.** Linear uses muted UI tones and lets typography do the emotional work. Match that — restrained palette, confident type for chapter cards.
- **One person, one voice.** A single narrator the entire way. Don't switch between a chirpy female VO for chapter 1 and a serious male VO for chapter 4. Pick one and live with them.

## 9. Concrete Opening Lines and Transitions (Quotable)

Real openings and transitions worth studying, with sources:

- **Loom:** *"Why don't you replace some of those meetings with a Loom?"* — character-driven cold open establishing the pain before the product appears. ([Loom](https://www.loom.com/community/product-demo))
- **Superhuman:** No spoken opening. The demo begins with a user pressing a shortcut and the UI doing something startlingly fast. ([Descript](https://www.descript.com/blog/article/software-demo-videos))
- **HubSpot:** *"Welcome to the HubSpot Customer Platform... all your customer-facing teams use the same system of record."* — a unified-platform claim made in the first 10 seconds. ([Descript](https://www.descript.com/blog/article/software-demo-videos))
- **Linear (Method):** *"There is a lost art of building true quality software."* — nostalgic, philosophical, immediately credible. ([Linear Method](https://linear.app/method))
- **Linear (home):** *"The system for modern product development."* — declares a category, doesn't ask permission. ([Linear](https://linear.app/))
- **A filmmaker-tuned hook to draft from:** *"Pre-production is where the film is actually made. So why does it still live in a spreadsheet?"*

Sample transitions to model:

- Artifact handoff: *"Here's the locked one-liner. The call sheet already knows."*
- Time jump: *"Three days out from the shoot."*
- Character handoff: *"Same board the director sees. Same board the first AD just opened on their phone."*

## 10. Anti-Patterns: What Not to Do

A short list, every one of which appears in bad SaaS demos:

- **Reading the UI back.** "Click the blue Create New button in the top right." If the user can see it, don't say it.
- **"Welcome to [Product]."** Opening with the product name burns the hook.
- **Logo animation cold open.** A 4-second swooshing logo is 4 seconds of churn.
- **The feature list.** "Storyvord has scheduling, budgeting, casting, scripting, AI breakdowns, and more." Lists kill story.
- **"You can also..." chains.** Three "you can also"s in a row and the viewer is gone.
- **Generic stock B-roll** (a woman laughing at a laptop) mixed with real UI. Pick one register and stay in it.
- **Founder talking head as the spine.** Founder cameos work as a 5-second outro flavor, never as the structure.
- **Pricing in the video.** The video sells the *feeling*; the landing page sells the plan.
- **Reading numbers on screen.** If "47 shots in 8 seconds" is on the chyron, the voice is doing something else.
- **Apologetic hedging.** "We think you might find this useful." No. Be opinionated.
- **Music that won't get out of the way.** The track should drop 6-8 dB under voice every time the narrator speaks.
- **More than one CTA at the end.** One ask, clearly stated, then silence.

## Quick Reference: A Creative Hub Chapter, Written to These Rules

> *(SFX: pen scratch on paper. Cut to a real script page being scanned.)*
>
> **VO:** "A script becomes a film in pre-production. Not on set."
>
> *(Beat. The script page lifts off and resolves into the Creative Hub breakdown view.)*
>
> **VO:** "Drop the script in. Creative Hub reads it the way an AD would — scenes, cast, locations, props, every gun and every dog."
>
> *(On-screen chyron: 112 scenes · 38 seconds. No voice.)*
>
> **VO:** "From here, the schedule writes itself. So does the call sheet for day one."
>
> *(Cut to the call sheet animating into place.)*
>
> **VO:** "Three days out from the shoot — and you've already prepped the whole film."

~95 words. ~40 seconds at 140 WPM. Hook, reveal, killer detail, workflow placement, time-jump transition.
